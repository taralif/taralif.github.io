Durable skills: the human side of the future of work

Durable skills are the human skills that don't expire: collaboration, critical thinking, communication, empathy, resilience. Tools and technical know-how change every few years. As AI takes on more of the routine work, the human part becomes the job: deciding what's true, working through disagreement, adapting when the plan changes.

These skills are also the hardest to teach and the hardest to prove. I've spent ten years working on both.

2017–2020 · Education Design Lab

Naming the skills, then making them teachable

Employers kept saying graduates lacked "soft skills." We broke eight 21st century skills down into sub-competencies and turned them into plug-and-play micro-credentials faculty could drop into Canvas. To show where skills overlap, I drew them as a metro map: each credential is a line, and shared skills are the transfer stations.

8 micro-credentials 2,000+ toolkit sign-ups 75 facilitators in the first cohort
2020–2023 · Sora Schools, founding PM

Making skills visible to students and parents

At a project-based online school, I built the LMS and the artifacts that showed what students could actually do. The transcript put abilities like Open Minded Discussion, Citizenship and Wayfinding on the same page as course credits, each with its own mastery level. When teachers asked for a feedback dashboard, I found that the real need was the weekly email to parents, so we automated that instead.

2026 · Infodemic, Assembly Code incubator

Practicing critical thinking inside a feed

Kids get their first feed around 7th grade, and nobody shows them how it works. In Infodemic, a whole class plays one round together. Everyone sees the same viral post and digs into the same evidence: search it, ask AI, check who's behind the account. Then they defend what they found, each write a community note, and rate each other's. The most helpful note gets filed.

Play it at playinfodemic.org →
